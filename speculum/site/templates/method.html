Method

Speculum describes; it does not accuse. Every reading below is a rate or a dollar figure computed the same way for every member, compared with the chamber and with the member's own party. Where a rule had to be chosen, the rule is written here.

Campaign cycles

House figures cover the current two-year cycle. Senate figures cover the senator's full six-year election cycle, because senators raise money across their whole term; where no six-year figure exists yet, the page says it shows the current two years only.

Counting lobbyist contributions once

LD-203 reports are filed by each lobbying organization and by each of its lobbyists, and the same check is often listed on several of them. A contribution counts once when it appears on several filings with the same date, amount, recipient and giver. Two identical lines on one filing both count: they are usually a primary and a general-election check.

Matching names to members

Reports name the honoree in free text. A name is matched to a member only when it is unambiguous: a senator's title must point to a senator; a first name, initial or common nickname given on the report must agree with the member's; "Last, First" is read as written; a line naming several people is left unattributed. A wrong match is treated as worse than no match, so some real contributions go unmatched. Every amount links to its filing.

Committee givers and interests

Each member's largest committee givers come from itemized receipts on FEC Schedule A. The member's own money is excluded: their personal contributions and loans, and transfers from committees named for them (their joint fundraising and leadership committees). Because only each member's largest givers are collected, interest totals are a floor, not a full count.

Senate roll calls

Senate votes come from Senate.gov, or from Voteview (UCLA) when Senate.gov does not respond to automated requests. Voteview also records the President's announced position on some votes; it is excluded from every tally. Senators are matched by their ICPSR identifiers.

The Dependents

Decisions are chosen by fixed rules applied to official vote titles, never by topic or party. Each link from a decision to an outcome carries an evidence grade: Documented (the source ties the weapons or actions in question to specific harm), Estimated (authoritative aggregate figures), Associated (a pattern or allegation without direct linkage), or No record found. Money around a decision is compared within each party, with a control that leaves out members of the committees that oversee the area. Advocacy groups count when they lobbied on the measure or publicly urged senators on it, with their side taken from their own statements, the same rule for every side; their PAC gifts are counted from the PAC's own reports, so bundled gifts are included. Research is done with AI and published item by item, automatically, once an independent AI checker has opened the item's sources and confirmed they support it at its grade; items graded Associated are not published automatically. Each item states one fact and carries the passage of its source that states it; a decision's record is checked field by field, each field against the sources it cites, and a chain is published once the fields saying what the measure did and what became of it have passed. An estimate of benefits is shown only beside the estimate of costs from the same analysis. When a long source is checked, the checker reads its opening and every passage containing the item's figures and quoted words. An item that does not pass is corrected once by the drafter, which may only narrow or remove what the checker found unsupported, never add to it, and is checked again; if it fails again it stays off the site. Research is written as a record, in the third person; an item that narrates its own research (“I found”) is restated once, changing no fact, and checked again. Readers can dispute any item with a source, which withdraws it until it is checked again.

Themes and their rules. Foreign arms sales: every Senate roll call on a joint resolution disapproving a proposed foreign arms sale under the Arms Export Control Act; money around the vote is from companies on the SIPRI Top 100 arms producers list. Regulations overturned: every roll call, in either chamber, on a joint resolution disapproving an agency rule under the Congressional Review Act, whoever sponsored it and whatever the rule concerned. What a rule was expected to do comes from the agency's own analysis, benefits and costs side by side, and is graded Estimated: a forecast, not a record of what followed. Budget reconciliation: every roll call on a bill titled as providing for reconciliation pursuant to a budget resolution (2 U.S.C. 641); the Congressional Budget Office's estimates of its effect on deficits and on households by income are shown side by side, graded Estimated. Ending national emergencies: every roll call on a joint resolution terminating a national emergency under the National Emergencies Act (50 U.S.C. 1622). War powers: every roll call on a resolution or bill directing the removal of United States Armed Forces from hostilities (50 U.S.C. 1544(c) and 1546a). For these four themes, a measure's House and Senate votes form one chain, and money around each chamber's last recorded vote is from the organizations whose own quarterly lobbying reports (LD-2) name the measure, given to members from the start of the reporting years through that vote (LD-203); the reports do not say which side an organization took. Research is drafted first for measures that became law, oldest first.

Where the money goes, and shared funders

Three measures, each from one kind of filing, each computed the same way for every member and party, and each recomputed every night. Money is grouped by each member's own party, so the independents appear on their own; votes count independents with the party they caucus with, because a party's majority on a vote includes them.

Campaign money by source adds up the receipts of every sitting member's principal campaign committee for the current cycle, as reported to the FEC: small individual gifts (unitemized, under $200), larger individual gifts, PACs and other non-party committees, party committees, and the candidate's own money. "Other" is what remains of total receipts (for example transfers and refunds).

Money from organizations that lobby adds up the federal campaign contributions that lobbying registrants, their PACs and their lobbyists report twice a year (LD-203) to sitting members, after the duplicate-removal described above. Each organization is placed in issue areas by what it lobbies on: the issue codes on its own quarterly lobbying reports (LD-2), weighted by the money on each report, so an organization that lobbies mostly on defense counts mostly toward defense. Lobbying firms, which report for many clients, are shown as their own group rather than spread across their clients' issues. Organizations with no lobbying report listing issue areas are shown as not placed.

Lobbying spending adds up quarterly lobbying reports (LD-2) since January 2025. The latest version of each report counts (amendments replace originals). A lobbying firm reports what a client paid it; an organization lobbying for itself reports what it spent, which under the Lobbying Disclosure Act guidance includes what it paid outside firms, so when an organization reports for itself, firms' reports for it are not added again. The lobbying records give each firm its own entry for the same client, so that match is made on the organization's name, ignoring case, punctuation and endings such as Inc. or LLC; a client named differently by a firm (a subsidiary, say) is not matched, so the totals can run slightly high. For 2025 they come to within about 3% of independent counts of total lobbying. Registrations, which carry no amounts, are not counted. A report lists issue areas but not dollars per area, so its amount is split equally across the areas it lists. Reports of $0 or under the reporting threshold add nothing.

Shared funders counts the organizations in the second measure: how many gave to at least one Democrat and at least one Republican, how many to members of only one party, and how much of the money came from those giving to both, for all organizations, for those giving $10,000 or more, and for the 100 largest. Gifts to independents count in totals but do not make an organization count as giving to both.

What it does not say. These measures count money and where it went. They do not say what it bought, and they do not compare the parties' votes; contributions and lobbying are legal and reported.

Who lobbies each bill

Every quarterly lobbying report (LD-2) describes what was lobbied on, in the lobbyists' own words, and often names bills. A bill is counted as lobbied by an organization when a report for that organization names it ("H.R. 1", "S. 2296", "S.J.Res. 26"). Senate bills are read only with their period ("S. 12"), so stray capitals are not taken for bills. Reports from 2025 and 2026 are read as naming bills of the 119th Congress; a mention in a quarter that ended before the bill was introduced is left out, since it refers to an earlier Congress's bill of the same number.

Reports say which bills were lobbied on, not which side the organization took or how much it spent on each bill, so neither is shown. Organizations are counted once per bill, by name. A bill gets a page when at least {{ bill_min_orgs }} organizations named it, or when it had a roll call and anyone named it.

Former officials. Each report lists its lobbyists and any covered government post they held (for example a congressional staff or executive-branch position), as they report it. A bill's list counts lobbyists who named it on a report and list such a post. Filers often put their current private job on this line instead ("Director of Government Relations", "Lobbyist"), so an entry counts only when it names a government office or body: a member of Congress, a chamber or committee, the White House, a department, agency, court or the military. Entries that name only a job title are left out, and a few real posts written without an office are left out with them; an occasional private title that names an office may remain.

Money to the members who voted. For each roll call, the federal campaign contributions (LD-203) from the organizations that lobbied the bill and file their own lobbying reports, to the members who voted yea and to those who voted nay. Every contribution reported since 2025 counts, before or after the vote. Contributions from lobbying firms are not counted, since a firm works for many clients.

Sectors. An organization is placed by its own description of its business on its lobbying registration, read against the rules below in order; the first rule whose words appear wins. When the description is blank or matches nothing, its name is read the same way. An organization the filing marks as a government entity is placed under governments. Two rules come first: a description that states a public mission (social justice, civil rights, human rights, the public interest, consumer advocacy or protection, education on a subject) is placed with advocacy groups and nonprofits unless it says the organization is an association or represents members or an industry; and with no description, a name that says what the organization stands for ("Center for", "Americans for", "Citizens for", "Campaign for", "People for", "Friends of") is read the same way. An organization's descriptions can differ from report to report, so its sector is the one most of its quarters give it. A word ending in * matches any word that starts with it. Sectors are grouped as business, labor unions, advocacy and nonprofits, education and research, and governments.

Cases

A case follows one decision through the record from start to finish. Its dated facts (what the rule would have done, the votes, the law) each link their source: the official roll call, the law as printed, or the agency's own announcement and estimates, which are forecasts, not results. The rest is computed every night by the site's rules: the organizations that named the measure on quarterly lobbying reports (LD-2), listed by sector and never by side, since a report does not say which side an organization took; whom those that file their own reports funded among the members who voted, by the Said & Done rule; and the lobbyists on those reports who list a past post at a financial regulator (the OCC, FDIC, Federal Reserve, CFPB, Treasury, SEC, NCUA or FHFA) or on a congressional banking committee, as they reported it, with the organizations they lobbied for. Reports that describe a measure in words without its number are not yet counted; each case says how many there are. A case is never dropped because of what it shows.

Said & Done

Each Said & Done measure sets one kind of filing against another by a fixed rule. The first, Both sides of the vote, takes the organizations that named a bill on a lobbying report (their own, or one a firm filed for them) and that file their own reports, and shows whom they funded on each side of its final vote. The deciding vote is, in each chamber, the last roll call on the measure itself (passage, adoption, agreeing to the other chamber's amendment or a conference report, or passage over a veto); votes on rules, motions, amendments and cloture are not counted. Contributions are those an organization or its PAC reported on LD-203 for the years read, before or after the vote; contributions a lobbyist reports as their own are left out, and so are lobbying firms. Findings

The Findings page holds a fixed list of measures, recomputed every night. A measure is added only with a note here, and never dropped because of what it shows.

  • Party agreement on votes. For each roll call (quorum calls left out), whether a majority of Democrats and a majority of Republicans voted on the same side, counting yea and nay only; a tie within a party counts as no majority. A roll call counts as solidly opposed when at least 90% of each party voted together, on opposite sides. "Bills and resolutions" are votes on passage, suspension of the rules and passage, joint resolutions, resolutions, concurring, conference reports and vetoes. Independents count with the party they caucus with; a former member no longer on the roster counts by their party at the vote.
  • Shared funders. Of the money organizations that lobby reported giving to sitting members (LD-203), the share from organizations that gave to at least one Democrat and at least one Republican.
  • Campaign money by source. Small gifts and PAC money as shares of each party's receipts in each chamber, as on the Money page.
  • Issue areas and seats. The number of issue areas in which Republican members' share of the two parties' money from organizations that lobby exceeds Republicans' share of the two parties' seats.
  • Largest giver. The organization that lobbies with the largest reported contributions to sitting members.
  • Lobbying spending. The total on quarterly lobbying reports since 2025, and the three largest issue areas.
  • Who lobbies bills. Every organization that named a bill with a page on a lobbying report, counted once by name, by sector group; and, for the bill named by the most organizations, how many did and how many of its lobbyists list a former government post.
  • Votes on blocking arms sales. The Senate roll calls on arms sales in The Dependents, how many passed, and the most votes cast to advance one.

Lame-duck watch

Members not returning in the next Congress are listed from the House Press Gallery's record and called races. Each one's votes after Election Day are compared with their own record before it; a change is shown once they have cast five party-splitting votes since.

Known limits

  • A high share of PAC money or a high party-unity rate is not evidence of wrongdoing. They describe how a member is funded and how they vote.
  • Campaign figures arrive at the pace the FEC's request limits allow; a night that cannot reach a member carries their last figures forward and says so.
  • Sectors are assigned by fixed word rules from each organization's own description of its business (the table under Who lobbies each bill). The rules place most organizations well, but a description can mislead them: a medical society may land in education, a nonprofit named "society" in trade groups. Sector shares are close readings, not exact counts.
  • Former government posts are counted from what lobbyists write on their reports, kept only when the entry names a government office; the count can miss a real post written without one, and keep an occasional private title that names one.
  • Readings that need coded funder positions (Independence, Exposure, Timing) are not shown until those positions have been reviewed by people.

Archive and corrections

Each night every raw file fetched from a source is saved untouched, hashed with SHA-256, and listed in a manifest attached to that night's release, so any figure can be traced to the exact bytes it came from. The normalized files built from them are committed each night to the project's public repository; a file is stored again only when its content changed, and every earlier version stays in the repository's history, listed by SHA-256 in that night's index. Fixes are made in the rules that read the files, never by editing saved data, and every fix is logged on the corrections page.
